# Arthritis

## Overview
Arthritis is a condition that causes inflammation, swelling, and tenderness in one or more joints. Joints are the areas where two or more bones meet, such as the knees, hips, fingers, and shoulders. In arthritis, the joints can become inflamed, leading to pain, stiffness, swelling, and difficulty moving. Over time, certain types of arthritis can cause damage to the cartilage, bones, and other tissues surrounding the joints. Cartilage is the smooth tissue that covers the ends of bones and helps them move against each other without friction.

## Common types of arthritis
Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common type of arthritis. It occurs when the protective cartilage covering the ends of bones gradually wears down. This can cause pain, stiffness, and reduced movement. It commonly affects the knees, hips, hands, and spine. Risk factors include aging, joint injuries, obesity, and genetics. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the lining of the joints (synovium), causing inflammation. This can lead to joint pain, swelling, stiffness, and eventually joint damage. It often affects the same joints on both sides of the body, particularly the hands, wrists, and feet.

## Other types of arthritis
Gout is a form of inflammatory arthritis caused by a buildup of uric acid crystals in the joints. It commonly affects the big toe and can cause sudden episodes of severe pain, redness, and swelling. Psoriatic arthritis is an inflammatory arthritis associated with psoriasis, a skin condition that causes red, scaly patches. It can affect the joints, fingers, toes, and areas where tendons attach to bones.

## Symptoms
Common symptoms of arthritis include joint pain or tenderness; swelling and inflammation around the joints; joint stiffness, especially in the morning or after periods of inactivity; redness and warmth around affected joints; decreased range of motion and difficulty moving affected joints; and fatigue, particularly in inflammatory types such as rheumatoid arthritis. Symptoms may come and go. Some people experience periods of increased symptoms called flares, followed by periods when symptoms improve.

## Causes and risk factors
The causes depend on the type of arthritis. Common risk factors include age (the risk of osteoarthritis increases with age), genetics (family history), previous joint injuries from sports, accidents, or repetitive movements, obesity (excess body weight stresses weight-bearing joints such as the knees and hips), and autoimmune conditions (an overactive immune system can mistakenly attack healthy joint tissues, as in rheumatoid arthritis).

## Diagnosis
Arthritis is diagnosed through a combination of medical history, physical examination, and diagnostic testing. Healthcare providers may use a physical examination to check for joint swelling, tenderness, redness, warmth, and limited movement; blood tests to identify signs of inflammation or antibodies associated with certain types of arthritis; X-rays to identify joint damage, cartilage loss, and changes in bone structure; MRI or ultrasound for more detailed images of joint tissues and inflammation; and joint fluid analysis to check for infection, inflammation, or uric acid crystals.

## Lifestyle changes
- Exercise regularly. Walking, swimming, and cycling are low-impact activities that help improve joint mobility.
- Maintain a healthy weight to reduce pressure on the joints.
- Eat a balanced diet: fatty fish (salmon, sardines, tuna) contain omega-3 fatty acids, which may help reduce inflammation; leafy greens (spinach, kale, broccoli) provide vitamins and antioxidants; berries (strawberries, blueberries, raspberries) contain antioxidants; nuts and seeds (walnuts, almonds, chia seeds, flaxseeds) provide healthy fats; whole grains (oats, brown rice, whole wheat) provide fiber.
- Avoid smoking, as it can increase the risk of rheumatoid arthritis and worsen symptoms.
- Get adequate sleep and allow time for rest, particularly during flare-ups.
- Protect joints by avoiding repetitive movements and using proper body mechanics.

## Medications and treatment
There is no universal cure for arthritis, but treatments can help manage symptoms, improve mobility, and prevent or slow joint damage. Treatment depends on the specific type of arthritis. NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) such as ibuprofen and naproxen help reduce pain and inflammation. Corticosteroids help reduce inflammation and may be prescribed during severe flare-ups. DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) such as methotrexate help slow disease progression and prevent joint damage in rheumatoid arthritis. Biologic medications target specific parts of the immune system responsible for inflammation, particularly in autoimmune forms of arthritis. Physical therapy helps strengthen muscles surrounding the joints, improve flexibility, and maintain mobility. In severe cases, damaged joints (commonly the hip or knee) may be replaced with joint replacement surgery to improve movement and reduce pain.
