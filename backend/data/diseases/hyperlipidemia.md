# Hyperlipidemia

## Overview
Hyperlipidemia, also known as high cholesterol, is a condition in which there are elevated levels of fats (lipids) in the blood. These fats include cholesterol and triglycerides. Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance that the body needs to build healthy cells, produce hormones, and make vitamin D. However, having too much cholesterol in the blood can increase the risk of health conditions.

## Types of cholesterol
There are two main types of cholesterol. LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein) is the "bad" cholesterol. It carries cholesterol throughout the body, and high levels of LDL can cause cholesterol to build up along artery walls, forming plaques that narrow or block blood vessels. HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein) is the "good" cholesterol. It helps remove excess cholesterol from the bloodstream and carries it back to the liver, where it can be processed and removed.

## Triglycerides
Triglycerides are a type of fat in the blood that stores excess calories for energy. High triglyceride levels can also increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, particularly when combined with low HDL or high LDL.

## Types of hyperlipidemia
Primary hyperlipidemia is usually caused by genetic factors or inherited conditions that affect how the body produces or processes cholesterol. A family history of high cholesterol can increase the risk. Secondary hyperlipidemia develops due to underlying medical conditions, certain medications, or lifestyle factors. Conditions such as diabetes, hypothyroidism, and kidney disease can contribute to elevated cholesterol levels.

## Symptoms
Hyperlipidemia typically does not cause noticeable symptoms. Most people do not know they have high cholesterol until they get a blood test. Over time, excess cholesterol can accumulate in artery walls, causing a condition called atherosclerosis. Atherosclerosis narrows the arteries and restricts blood flow, increasing the risk of heart attack and stroke.

## Diagnosis
Hyperlipidemia is diagnosed through a blood test called a lipid panel (lipid profile). This test measures total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides. Cholesterol levels are measured in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL).

## Desirable cholesterol levels
Some general desirable levels for adults include: total cholesterol below 200 mg/dL; LDL cholesterol below 100 mg/dL; HDL cholesterol at least 40 mg/dL in men and 50 mg/dL in women; triglycerides below 150 mg/dL. Optimal levels can vary depending on a person's age, medical history, and cardiovascular risk.

## Lifestyle changes
- Quit smoking.
- Exercise regularly. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week.
- Maintain a healthy weight.
- Eat a heart-healthy diet: leafy greens (spinach, kale) provide fiber and nutrients; fruits (apples, berries, citrus) provide fiber and antioxidants; nuts and seeds (almonds, walnuts, chia seeds, flaxseeds) contain healthy fats and fiber; whole grains (oats, brown rice, whole wheat) provide soluble fiber, which helps lower LDL cholesterol; healthy fats (olive oil, avocados, fatty fish such as salmon) provide unsaturated fats and omega-3 fatty acids.
- Limit foods high in saturated and trans fats, such as fried foods, processed meats, butter, and full-fat dairy products.
- Limit excessive alcohol consumption, especially if triglyceride levels are elevated.
